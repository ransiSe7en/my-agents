# Rubric-aligned presentations

Use the current assignment brief and marking rubric for requirements. Use the user's final or latest edited deck for their content and style. Treat earlier drafts as evidence only where they help. Use the available Presentations skill for PowerPoint authoring, notes, and visual checks.

## Intake

Read the supplied materials first, including the full rubric and academic integrity rules. Identify unanswered questions that could change the deck, the demo, or the claims. Use the user-input tool for a short batch. Offer multiple choice for format and pacing, and free text for personal experience. Ask only for gaps. If a first answer reveals a material gap, ask a targeted follow-up. Work on independent parts while waiting.

- Brief and audience: What are the exact assessment criteria and highest-band descriptors? Who will assess or watch? What are the required sections, slide count or template, hard speaking limit, Q&A limit, and submission format? Does demo time count? What assistance is allowed?
- Project: What problem made the project necessary, and who is affected? What does each stakeholder need? What was your part, what was shared, and what belonged to others?
- Substance: Which decision, trade-off, or technical challenge best shows your judgement? Which discipline concept or course helped in practice? What evidence can you show, what did testing actually establish, and what remains open?
- Demo: What product or artefact will you show outside the slides? What will you open, what action will you take, and what should the audience notice? What account, environment, and data are approved for showing? Is there a recording or screenshot for a fallback? Which slides or artefacts does the rubric explicitly require?
- Reflection: What changed in how you work? Ask for an actual example of an initial assumption, feedback or a result that challenged it, what you did differently, and what you would do next.
- Delivery: Are notes wanted as cues or a script, and may a backup deck or appendix accompany the assessed deck?

Never invent first-person experiences, feedback, metrics, or outcomes. If the brief permits AI for editing only, work from the user's own draft, notes, and answers. Help them identify missing evidence and refine what they supply within that boundary.

## Convert the rubric into a deck and delivery plan

Map each criterion to its exact descriptor, where it is covered, what the presenter will explain or demonstrate, and the evidence for it. Use the mapping to find thin areas and guide questions. A matching heading alone does not satisfy a criterion. Preserve required section order, slide count, and submission rules. Distinguish mandatory instructions from suggested timing and examples. If suggested times exceed the hard limit, fit the combined talk, demo, and transitions into that limit and place Q&A where the brief requires.

When the brief specifies sections without a slide count, choose the count from the rubric, the total speaking time, and how much time the demo and transitions need. Combine closely related points when they can be explained clearly together. Add another slide when it gives essential evidence room to be read or gives the presenter a clear cue to switch to the product. If the user requests an exact count, fit the demo cue within that count. Keep optional backup material in a separate deck when the user wants it and the submission rules permit it.

Start with the project-level problem and stakeholder needs. Give individual features their own role as evidence of a decision or outcome. Use diagrams for how the system works, a product UI or output for what the audience should see, and a scoped result for what the evidence established. Use pull request or code screenshots when the rubric explicitly calls for them or reading the code is central to understanding a technical decision. Otherwise cite them in the notes if they substantiate ownership or implementation. Prefer a legible product artefact or demo to a commit history.

Keep explanations, optional detail, transitions, timing, and sources in actual PowerPoint speaker notes when requested. Keep slides readable at presentation size, with one main point and a speaking purpose per visual. Verify who owns the work, the scope of each result, and which evidence can be shown to this audience.

## Plan a separate product demonstration

An artefact showcase slide can introduce the demonstration. Give it a brief workflow or the outcome to watch for. In the notes, mark the moment to switch to the product, the pre-opened view, the exact action sequence, the expected evidence, the allotted time, and where to return in the deck. Include a permitted fallback for a slow, unavailable, or unsuitable live environment. Treat viewing and live actions separately and use the environment authorised for each. When switching outside PowerPoint, keep presenter notes and unrelated or sensitive content out of the audience's view.

Before delivering, verify the requested count, order, readable visuals, actual speaker notes, cumulative timing including the demo and transitions, and criterion coverage. Report any material evidence gaps or timing assumptions without predicting a grade. Read [the worked example](references/internship-example.md) when using this approach for an internship showcase.
